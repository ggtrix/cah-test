    The caveat we publish ourselves

    The exploited-vulnerability headline is mostly one old library.

    What the two records mean, value by value

    Pick a setting to see what a receiving mail server does when someone forges your address. Your report names the settings your domain uses.

    DMARC _dmarc.yourhospital.org

    Tells receiving mail servers what to do with a message that uses your name in the From line but fails the authentication check. The key part is the p= policy.

    Forged mail

    SPF yourhospital.org

    Lists the servers allowed to send your mail. The all at the end decides what happens to every server not on the list — which is where a forger sends from.

    Unlisted server

    Why you need both. SPF checks the hidden sender address that mail servers pass between themselves — recipients never see it. DMARC checks the From address people do see, and only passes when SPF (or DKIM, a cryptographic signature) passes for that same domain. That link is what stops a forger putting your hospital’s name in the From line. SPF on its own does not.

    The other parts of each record
    v=DMARC1
    Marks the record as DMARC. It must come first.
    rua=
    Where daily summary reports are sent. Without it, nobody sees who is sending as you.
    pct=
    The share of failing mail the policy applies to. The default is 100; below that, the rest is treated one step more leniently.
    sp=
    The policy for subdomains such as mail.yourhospital.org. If absent, they follow p=.
    adkim= aspf=
    How closely the From domain must match: r (relaxed, the default) or s (strict).
    v=spf1
    Marks the record as SPF. A domain may have only one; two break it.
    include:
    Trusts another service’s senders, such as Microsoft 365 or Google Workspace.
    ip4: ip6: mx a
    Trusts specific addresses, or the domain’s own mail and web servers.
    redirect=
    Uses another domain’s SPF record instead of its own list.

    The check reads two public DNS records for your domain (DMARC and SPF). Nothing on your network is contacted. We keep your email address and the domain you checked so we can send the report and follow up if you ask for help.

    Why these hospitals

    A critical access hospital has no second site to fail over to.

    The designation exists because these hospitals are the only one for miles. That is also what makes an outage at one of them different from an outage anywhere else.

    What the designation means

    A critical access hospital has 25 or fewer acute inpatient beds, keeps a 24/7 emergency department, averages a 96-hour length of stay, and sits more than 35 miles from another hospital — 15 in mountainous terrain. Congress created the designation in the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 after more than 400 rural hospitals closed.

    Rural Health Information Hub

    Recovery takes weeks, not days

    Across 43 rural and 117 urban hospitals attacked between 2016 and 2021, admissions and revenue took two to three weeks to return to pre-attack levels. The median nearest unaffected rural hospital was over 30 minutes away, against under 10 minutes for urban ones.

    Neprash et al., 2024 — The Journal of Rural Health

    The harm lands next door

    At an emergency department adjacent to a hospital under ransomware attack, ambulance arrivals rose 35.2%, door-to-room times 47.6%, stroke code activations 74.6% and patients leaving without being seen 127.8%. The authors conclude these attacks should be treated as regional disasters.

    Dameff et al., 2023 — JAMA Network Open

    External figures are attributed to their source and were checked against it. Everything else on this page is SureShield's own measurement of 977 critical access hospital domains and 15,037 internet-facing subdomains.

    How we evaluated cyber resilience

    From a public domain to a risk band, in seven steps.

    What we look at, what we never touch, and why a single forgotten subdomain can set a hospital’s score — explained one step at a time, with live examples.

    Common questions

    What exactly did you scan?

    Only what is published to the internet: DNS records, TLS certificates, banners on open ports and the public face of web applications. Nothing was exploited, no account was accessed and no system was touched. The same view an attacker gets for free before they decide whether to bother.

    Why score on the worst subdomain rather than an average?

    Because an attacker does not average. One forgotten server with Remote Desktop open is the way in, however well the other thirty are run. The walk-through above lets you try it: select one subdomain to see how fixing it moves the score.

    How current is this?

    Vulnerability data is checked against the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and FIRST EPSS, both current to 2026-09-08. A large share of the scored dataset comes from the oldest scan source and has not been refreshed since, so treat the picture as a floor rather than a snapshot of today.

    Will you name our hospital publicly?

    No. The three reports and the rest of this page are aggregate only. The lookup names a hospital back only to someone who already typed its name or domain, and never shows what the findings are. Peer comparisons are anonymized by design and revenue band is never displayed. An individual report goes only to a verified representative of that hospital.

    We are small. Does that not make us a smaller target?

    The data says no. Bed count barely correlates with risk at all (Spearman 0.098), and neither CMS star rating nor emergency department status separates the sector. What predicts risk is how many subdomains you have facing the internet.

    Our hospital is not in the lookup. Why?

    The assessment covers 977 domains, which is 86.0% of the critical access hospitals in the registry we hold. The rest either had no domain we could confidently match or did not return data when scanned.

    What would we actually fix first?

    For most hospitals here, the cheapest meaningful change is email authentication: 352 domains are weak on DMARC and SPF at the same time, and that is a DNS record edit, not a procurement cycle. After that, close or put behind a VPN whatever admin and database ports are open, and retire the DNS records pointing at infrastructure you no longer run.

      How we evaluated cyber resilience 1 / 7
      Step 1

      We look from the outside, the way an attacker would

      Everything in this assessment comes from what a hospital already publishes to the internet. Nobody logged in, nothing was exploited, and no system was touched.

      It is the same view anyone gets for free before deciding whether a hospital is worth attacking — which is exactly why it is worth seeing first.

      What we read

      • DNS records
      • TLS certificates
      • Open ports and the banners behind them
      • The public face of websites and portals
      • Email authentication records

      What we never do

      • Log in anywhere
      • Exploit a flaw
      • Go past a normal visit
      • Look inside the network
      • Name a hospital publicly
      Step 2

      One domain can hide many subdomains

      Each assessment starts from a hospital’s web domain and finds every internet-facing subdomain under it, such as portal.yourhospital.org or mail.yourhospital.org. Several subdomains can share one IP address; each one is checked and scored on its own.

      A small independent hospital usually has a handful. A health system’s shared domain can carry dozens, and a hospital on that domain sits behind all of them. Every subdomain is another door.

      Step 4

      A flaw being used today outranks one that is merely severe on paper

      A sector that cannot patch everything needs to know what attackers are actually using. So known vulnerabilities are ranked by real-world evidence, not by how bad they could be in theory.

      Evidence comes from the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and FIRST’s Exploit Prediction Scoring System, both current to .

      Step 5

      The worst subdomain sets the score

      An attacker does not average. One forgotten server is the way in, however well the other subdomains are run. So a hospital’s headline score is the score of its worst internet-facing subdomain.
